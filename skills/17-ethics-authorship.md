## Why this exists

Authorship disputes happen at submission, which is the one moment when they cannot be resolved
calmly. Everyone has been working for a year on an assumption nobody stated, and the
assumptions do not match. In a three-tier lab this is sharper than usual: an REU student who
ran the sweeps, an MS student who built the data pipeline, and a PhD student who wrote the
paper have genuinely different claims, and none of them are obvious. Writing it down early
costs an hour. Not writing it down costs a relationship.

## External material

The [CRediT taxonomy](https://credit.niso.org/) — fourteen defined contributor roles, and the
standard most journals now ask for directly. The [ICMJE authorship
criteria](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html)
for the four-part test of what makes someone an author rather than an acknowledgement. Both
define terms. Neither resolves your specific case, which is the point of the mechanic.

## Governing part

None.

## The mechanic

For a paper you are actually working on:

1. List **everyone who has touched it.** Including the undergraduate who ran the sweeps.
   Including the labmate who fixed your solver configuration. The list is longer than you think.
2. Map each person to CRediT roles.
3. Apply the ICMJE test to decide author versus acknowledgement. Write the reason.
4. State the order, and justify it in one sentence.
5. Name the corresponding author.
6. **Send it to all of them.** This step is the assignment. A contribution statement nobody
   has seen is a private opinion.

## Deliverable

The statement, plus evidence it was circulated — a timestamp, a thread, a forwarded message.

## Competency check

- [ ] Every contributor named, including students at other tiers
- [ ] CRediT roles assigned per person
- [ ] Author versus acknowledgement decided with a stated reason
- [ ] Order justified in one sentence
- [ ] Corresponding author named
- [ ] Evidence of circulation
