## Why this exists

Figures are read first and often alone. Yours will be screenshotted into someone else's slide
deck, printed in greyscale by a reviewer, and looked at for four seconds by a programme
committee member deciding whether to read the abstract. A figure that needs the body text to
make sense fails all three. This is also the cheapest place in the whole curriculum to improve
how your work is received, because the fix is an hour and the effect is on every reader.

## External material

Rougier, Droettboom & Bourne, ["Ten Simple Rules for Better
Figures"](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1003833), PLOS
Computational Biology 2014. Short, free, and the best single thing published on this. It covers
principles. It does not know your data.

## Governing part

None. Note that skill 16 in this same milestone adds requirements to the same figure — read them

## The mechanic

Take one figure from **your own results** — ideally the one you expect to be the paper's headline.

- **The caption must stand alone.** Someone who has read no body text should understand what the
  figure shows, on what data, and what the takeaway is. If your caption is "Results of the
  optimisation", rewrite it.
- **Units on both axes.** MW, $/MWh, tonnes, years. Every time.
- **Survive greyscale.** Print it, or convert it. If two series become indistinguishable, colour
  was doing work that linestyle or marker should have been doing.
- **No more series than you can name in the caption.** If you have eleven ERCOT zones and can
  name three, plot three and aggregate the rest.

## Competency check

- [ ] Before and after both shown
- [ ] Caption comprehensible with no body text — test it on someone outside your subfield
- [ ] Units on both axes
- [ ] Greyscale conversion shown, series still distinguishable
- [ ] Colour is never the only channel carrying meaning
- [ ] Every plotted series is named in the caption
