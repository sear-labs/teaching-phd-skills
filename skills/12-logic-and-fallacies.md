## Why this exists

An argument can be perfectly valid and completely false, or true in every part and still
invalid. Keeping those apart is the content of a first-semester logic course, and it is the
tool you use to say *why* a paper's conclusion does not follow rather than just feeling that it
does not.

There is now a sharper reason. **A language model produces fluent, well-formed, invalid
reasoning at scale.** Its output has the cadence of a careful argument — the connectives are
right, the hedges are in the right places, the structure looks like reasoning — and fluency is
exactly the cue humans use to judge whether reasoning is sound. Your students are calibrated on
a signal that no longer tracks the thing it used to track.

## External material

The [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy on fallacies](https://iep.utm.edu/fallacy/) — free,
thorough, and organised so you can look one up rather than read it front to back.

If you want a book: Hurley, *A Concise Introduction to Logic*, chapters 1 and 3. Chapter 1 is
validity and soundness; chapter 3 is the informal fallacies. Nothing after that is needed here.

## The mechanic

Four distinctions, which cover most of what goes wrong in engineering papers:

- **Valid vs. sound.** Valid means the conclusion follows *if* the premises hold. Sound means
  valid *and* the premises are actually true. Most bad papers are valid and unsound.
- **Necessary vs. sufficient.** Storage is necessary for a high-renewables grid; it is not
  sufficient. Conflating these produces most overclaiming in energy modelling.
- **Correlation, causation, and the third thing.** Including the case where your model *assumed*
  the causal direction and then recovered it, which is not evidence.
- **Base rates.** A test, a screen, or a heuristic that is "90% accurate" tells you almost
  nothing until you know how rare the thing is.

Then find **three real fallacies** — one in each place:

1. **A published paper in your area.** They are there. Read for the inference, not the topic.
2. **Your own draft.** This one is not optional and it is the point of the exercise.
3. **AI output.** Ask a model something in your field where you know the answer is contested,
   and read what it produces for the *form* of the reasoning rather than the content.

For each: quote it, name the failure, and say what would have to be added to repair it.
