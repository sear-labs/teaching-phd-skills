## The rules

**Four hours. A hard cap.** Not a target, not a minimum. If you hit four hours with things
unfinished, stop and tell us what was left — that is often a more useful submission than a
finished one. If you find yourself at hour eight, we set the task badly.

**Use AI, and tell us how.** Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, whatever you use. This is not a trap and
there is no purity test — AI-assisted work is how the lab works, and checking a model's output
is something we teach explicitly. We care that you can say where you leaned on it and where you
decided not to trust it.

**Look things up.** You are not expected to know what any of this means going in. Reading around
an unfamiliar problem until it makes sense is most of what research is, and it is one of the
things we are actually interested in seeing.

**There is nobody to ask.** Deliberately. Where something is ambiguous, make a call, write down
what you assumed, and keep going. Your assumptions are one of the things we will read.

## What to send back

### 1. Reproduce the output

The expected result is in the note that came with the folder — an objective value, a decision
vector, a metric, depending on the project.

Run it and tell us whether you got the same thing. **If you got something different, say so** —
a documented difference is a real result and we would rather have it than a match you are not
confident in. If you could not get it running at all, tell us how far you got and where it
stopped.

### 2. Tell us what this model is actually doing

Not the code — the problem. A few paragraphs:

- What is being optimized or computed, and why would anyone want to know it
- What do the decisions or outputs represent in the real world
- What would this answer mean to someone working in the field

You will have to look things up to write this, and that is the point. Cite what you read, even
if it was Wikipedia and a blog post — we would rather see an honest trail than a confident
paragraph from nowhere.

Being roughly right here is fine. Being interested is what we are reading for.

### 3. Write the README that would have let you do this in half the time

Assume the reader is you, four hours ago. What did you need to know, in what order, that was
not written down anywhere?

This is the piece we read most closely. It is hard to write a good one without having actually
understood what the code does.

### 4. List three things that would break on someone else's machine

A hard-coded path, an unpinned package, an assumption about where a file lives, a version that
matters. Three is the ask; more is welcome.

### 5. A short working note

Half a page, informally written:

- **What you assumed**, where the folder did not tell you something
- **What you tried that did not work**, and roughly what it cost you
- **How you used AI** — what you asked, what it got wrong, what you checked and how

Write this as you go rather than at the end. It is the part that tells us most about how you
work, and it is impossible to reconstruct convincingly afterwards.

**Send it however is easiest** — a zip, a repo link, a document. We are not marking the
packaging.

## What we are looking at

No hidden criteria, so here is the list:

- **Did you get curious about the problem.** Whether you went and found out what the thing
  actually does, and whether it interested you.
- **Can you get unfamiliar code running** without someone sitting next to you.
- **Can you explain it back** once you have.
- **Do you notice what is fragile** about somebody else's project.
- **Can you say plainly what you guessed, and what did not work.**
- **Did you use AI and check it.**

What we are **not** looking at: whether you already knew the domain, whether the code was
elegant, or whether you finished.
