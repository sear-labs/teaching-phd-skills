# M3 — Reading and Reasoning

**Tier:** Year 1–2

Evaluating somebody else's argument, finding where the field stops, spotting an inference that
fails, building a claim of your own, and saying it no more strongly than your evidence allows.

One spine holds the five together:

> A claim needs reasons and evidence. The link between them is a **warrant**. Warrants are
> almost always unstated — and the unstated warrant is where an argument actually breaks.

That is true of the papers you read and of the ones you write, which is why reading and
reasoning sit in the same milestone rather than in two.

**Take it early.** It is tiered Year 1–2 rather than Year 2 because everything in M4 and M5
lands better afterwards. A contribution claim, a referee report, and a rebuttal are the same
skill wearing different clothes, and this is where that skill is actually taught.

## On "critical thinking"

You will be asked whether your training covered this — in interviews, on fellowship
applications, by your own committee. The answer is yes, and the useful version of the answer is
specific, because the phrase on its own means almost nothing and everybody claims it.

This milestone is where it lives, and each skill teaches a different part of it:

- **Analysis** — taking an argument apart: what is claimed, what is assumed, what is actually
  shown *(reading and critiquing papers)*
- **Evaluation** — judging whether an inference holds, including when it is fluent and wrong
  *(logic and its failures)*
- **Synthesis** — putting fifteen papers on one axis to see where the field stops
  *(literature review and gap-finding)*
- **Creative thinking** — generating framings and choosing between them, rather than defaulting
  to the first one you saw *(literature review and gap-finding)*
- **Constructing an argument** — a claim with reasons, evidence, and a warrant you can state
  *(claim, reason, evidence · saying exactly what you claim)*

Say that instead of the phrase. It is more impressive, and it is true.

### Order

**Claim, reason, evidence comes before the other two writing skills** — both use its output, and
*saying exactly what you claim* audits the claim it produces.

You need some writing of your own for the last two. A conference paper, a proposal section or a
chapter fragment is plenty; it does not have to be finished or good. If you have nothing at all
written yet, take the three reading skills first and come back.
