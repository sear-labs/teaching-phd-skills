# What we don't grade

Nothing on this page is assessed. There is no assignment, no checklist, no submission, and it
will never appear in your milestone record.

It is here because the twenty-eight graded skills are the ones every researcher needs, and these
are the ones that would make you unusually good at this particular kind of work. They did not
make the curriculum because they are not *PhD-basic* — a perfectly competent researcher can go a
whole career without them. The best ones don't.

Read one when you are stuck, curious, or procrastinating on something worse.

---

## Philosophy of science

**Why it matters here:** you build models of systems you cannot run experiments on. You will
never have a control group for the Texas grid. So the question of what counts as evidence for a
claim about a system you can only simulate is not abstract for you — it is your entire
methodology, and it has a literature.

Underdetermination, model validation, and what it means for a model to be "right" when every
model is wrong. Start with Popper on falsification, then Kuhn on why fields resist it. If you
read one thing: Box's "all models are wrong, some are useful" essay, which is four pages and
better than its famous sentence.

## The normative content of your modelling choices

**Why it matters here:** a discount rate is a claim about how much future people matter. Three
percent and seven percent are different moral positions, and the choice between them was argued
out by economists and philosophers, not engineers.

The same is true of your objective function, which decides whose welfare counts; your system
boundary, which decides whose costs are invisible; and your spatial aggregation, which decides
whose congestion shows up in the results. None of these are bugs. They are choices, they are
defensible, and you should know you are making them.

Start with the Stern Review's discounting chapter against Nordhaus's response — the two sides
of the most consequential parameter argument in climate economics.

## Policy and institutional literacy

**Why it matters here:** you model ERCOT. Somebody decided ERCOT would be an energy-only market
with no capacity payment, and that decision was political, recent, and revisable. Knowing who
decides, through what process, and on what timescale changes which of your results are
actionable and which are interesting but inert.

PUCT, FERC, the ERCOT stakeholder process, and how a protocol revision request actually moves.
Read one PUCT docket end to end; it is more informative than any summary.

## History of your own field

**Why it matters here:** the grid looks the way it does for reasons, and most of them are
historical accidents that hardened. Rural electrification, PUHCA in 1935, the 1978 PURPA
opening, restructuring in the 1990s, and Texas's decision to stay outside the interconnections.
You will model constraints that exist because of a law passed in 1935.

## The economics of what you model

**Why it matters here:** externalities, market failure, and why a carbon price is a policy
instrument rather than a moral statement. If your optimisation minimises cost, you have adopted
a welfare framework — worth knowing which one, and what it does not capture.

## Intercultural and global collaboration

**Why it matters here:** this lab collaborates across countries and the supply chains you study
cross a dozen jurisdictions with different rules, different data conventions, and different
ideas about what a deadline means. This one is learned by doing and by paying attention, not by
reading, which is exactly why it cannot be a graded skill.
